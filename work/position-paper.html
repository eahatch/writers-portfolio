More Human than Non-Human: Exigence and Agency in Assemblage Theory

Alan Hatch · October 29, 2026

Position paper prepared for Dr. Michael Healy.

In this paper

  • Exigence
  • Agency
  • Assemblage
  • Assemblage in TPC
  • Conclusion
  • Works Cited

I’ve been both enjoying and struggling with the ideas of exigence and agency in the context of post-humanist philosophy since the idea was first introduced in class a couple of weeks ago. The idea of human “decentralization” in an Object Oriented Ontology seems well suited to this age of emergent AI behavior. But is Assemblage theory well suited to supplant the more traditional rhetorical frameworks as a model to describe the process of making social change through rhetorical artifacts? For me it all boils down to exigence and agency.

Exigence

According to the online version of Webster’s dictionary exigency is “that which is required in a particular situation”. For the sake of this conversation I would alter that statement to be “that which is required of a particular situation.” This definition stands, at least for a while, as we survey the evolution of exigence in modern rhetorical theory as it relates to TPC.

In Bitzer’s Rhetorical situation there exists a “natural context of persons, events, objects, relations, and an exigence which strongly invites utterance . . .” (5). In this context exigence a reaction to a situation where the need for change is recognized. The implication is that human perception is central to recognizing the need for change and the resulting rhetoric is an individual process.

In “Genre as Social Action,” Miller cites that “if genre represents action, it must involve situation and motive, because human action, whether symbolic or otherwise, is interpretable only against a context of situation and through the attributing of motives” (52). Here we see the idea of human perception’s role in exigence reinforced. But we also see the emerging role of context in exigency: “[b]ut at the level of the genre, motive becomes a conventionalized social purpose, or exigence, within the recurrent situation. In constructing discourse, we deal with purposes at several levels, not just one. We learn to adopt social motives as ways of satisfying private intentions through rhetorical action.” (162). So now we see that exigence is codified in a “conventionalized social purpose,” suggesting that context might have a greater role in exigence than previously described, at least in the conceptualization of rhetorical genre.

Later work by Edbauer proposes rhetorical situation in a social framework where it is “possible to situate the exigence's evocation within a wider context of affective ecologies comprised of material experiences and public feelings” (5). In Edbauer’s framing of “rhetorical ecologies” exigence is no longer a personal event for an individual rhetor, but a public reaction to a socially relevant situation. While this expands the traditional framework of the rhetorical situation, its still based on human perception, albeit social rather than individual.

Agency

Webster’s definition of agency is “the capacity, condition, or state of acting or of exerting power.” This definition strays somewhat from the traditional concept of agency being an intentional construct, which is interesting in its own right. Have the philosophical developments of the past 50 years changed the way we conceptualize agency? It seems that we may be adapting our core concepts to support philosophical trends which I find somewhat concerning.

In reviewing the articles by Bitzer, Edbauer and Miller, the concept of agency isn’t required as part of their frameworks because humans are the only agents in consideration. So agency, rather than taking the somewhat linear evolutionary track of exigence, makes a punctuated evolutionary turn when post-humanist concepts enter the conversation. Which is to say that at the point that we decentralize humans other non-human actors come into scope.

Assemblage

According to Thomas Nail’s explanation of Assemblage, it originated as a political theory to account for the complexities in social systems and explain their behavior. In their foundational work on assemblage, Deleuze and Guattari provide a framework that emphasizes a system as an “arrangement of heterogenous elements” thus dismissing the idea of systems being unified entities and advancing the idea that systems are composed of numerous entities that have unique and individual identities, but when presented in a system interact in patterns that can be recognized and classified” (22).

Assemblage “constructs or lays out a set of relations between self subsisting fragments—what Deleuze calls “singularities" (23). The distinction here is that the component parts, or singularities, of an assemblage have unique identities when considered on their own. This provides the ability to disassemble and reassemble different assemblages from the same component entities. In a modern technical context this could be likened to a tiger team, where experts from different subject matters form an assemblage to tackle a difficult problem or project. When the project is complete, the team disassociates back into the context of their original organizational structure, but the individual team members could be brought back together or assembled with different singularities more attuned to the task at hand.

An assemblage, along with its constituent singularities, is always in flux and can only be known at a given point in time according to “its collection of contingent features at a certain point in its incomplete process” (24). This presents a complex portrait of a group of interdependent actors who are influenced, not only, by one another, but location and even history. The relationship between these actors and resulting behavior of the system becomes extremely difficult to define.

Assemblage theory provides the idea of an “abstract machine” to help conceptualize the infinite number of actors and relationships in an assemblage. “The condition of an assemblage is abstract because it is not a thing or object that exists in the world, but rather something that lays out a set of relations wherein concrete elements and agencies appear” (24).

Additionally, there are “concrete elements” that are the components of the assemblage that do not belong to the “abstract machine.” They are the materialist constructs that are “the existing embodiment of the assemblage” (26). This might include people, houses, tanks, paper clips and documents.

Back to the tiger team, we now have an abstract machine defined not only by the relationship between the team members, but also how they interact with the other material objects in the assemblage. This system of objects and interaction yields a pattern that, in a given moment, can be understood and even classified. According to assemblage theory: “If we want to understand how an assemblage works, we do not ask what its essence is, but rather what it can do” (26).

When trying to understand complex systems, its often more productive to watch them behave rather than understanding the inner workings of the system. In fact, some systems are so complex, like large language models, that understanding their essence is out of reach of the scientist that created them. So instead we monitor key points of interest in the system to understand and potentially predict behavior. This is all to say that while assemblage theory recognizes both agency and exigence as part of the “abstract machine” that drives the assemblage, it’s purpose isn’t to understand or even identify those factors, instead it seeks to understand behavior.

Similar, however, to Edbauer’s work on rhetorical ecologies, assemblage insists on the social nature of meaning. “No one is subject to themselves alone; they are part of a larger third-person assemblage that arranges the conditioning relations and concrete elements in which the world of the agent is meaningful” (28). But assemblage differs in how it views agency.

Assemblage, in its original form, uses “deterritorialization” and “reterritorialization” to describe the factors that cause the assemblage to change over time, that encourage stability or decay, that recreate existing patterns or new patterns. This type of agency is viewed from a macro lens and does not have the resolution to describe the potentially limitless acts of individual or social agency that culminate in these changes. This is less of the agency that we consider as part of the rhetorical process and more of the agency involved in systems like equilibrium.

Assemblage in TPC

I’m using Johnson-Eilolaa and Selberb’s article “Technical Communication as Assemblage” as a point of reference for assemblage theory in TPC. I acknowledge this is not necessarily representative of the entire field of study, but hope to make some specific points that can be reapplied to other studies. While the previous articles had little to say about agency, agency is referenced eleven times in in “Technical Communication as Assemblage,” as the decentralization of humans in post-humanist thinking begs the question of agency. Conversely, exigence isn’t mentioned at all, which aligns with the idea that in an assemblage both exigence and agency are part of the “abstract machine” which is not intended to be understood: “[t]here are at least two major philosophical consequences . . . that Deleuze and Guattari use to develop their general logic of assemblages: the rejection of unity in favor of multiplicity, and the rejection of essence in favor of events” (Nail 22).

The authors argue that since our understanding of TPC has become more complex over the years, we can use assemblage as tool to describe the complex relations between the material and abstract elements in modern rhetorics: “[t]his is all to say that any sociotechnical system is a complicated context, making assigning agency a messy, imprecise, and controversial task” (84). But assemblage doesn’t attempt to ascribe agency at all other than to say it exists at some level within the construct of the assemblage: “[h]owever, as many have begun to recognize, agency may represent an oversimplified notion of interactions in complex sociotechnical systems” (83). In this approach we take our “hands off the wheel” of logic and suggest that the essence of the modern rhetorical situation is too complex to understand.

If we are to follow the path of assemblage, we should acknowledge that both exigence and agency are part of the “abstract machine,” and begin to look at how rhetorical systems behave rather than try to discover the essence of things like users, exigency and agency. Why deterritorialization and reterritorialization happens isn’t important, rather the outcomes of those changes become the focal point of assemblage. Simply put, agency no longer matters beyond the effects it produces as part of the larger assemblage. But the article continues to focus on the objects, users and agency as it builds a use case in maker spaces: “[m]akerspaces provide a material space that can help the TPC field evolve how it has theorized and enacted the user, especially as users relate to objects and questions of agency” (89).

The concluding remark of the article states that “[o]ur application in makerspaces illustrates what our approach to assemblage can buy technical communicators in practice, including analogical reasoning. In addition to asking what users want, asking what objects want requires us to re-think agency as always partial and sometimes out of control and agentless. It can also open us up to working at different scales and in oscillating degrees of territorialization” (95). While I agree that all those assertions were addressed in the article, I continue to suggest that in the context of assemblage, they simply don’t matter. Assemblage posits a system so complex that it can’t be understood at its component levels, while this study proposed to understand an assemblage at its component levels.

Conclusion

I believe that assemblage theory may not be a good fit for TPC based on Deleuze and Guattari (as explained by Nail). Because assemblage seeks to describe the emergent behavior of complex systems it becomes difficult to view the inner workings of those systems through the lens of assemblage as they relate to creating rhetorical artifacts. The scale is simply too large and the resolution too low. I further suggest that because of this same lack of resolution, the concepts of exigence and agency are reframed at a scale that is also not useful to conversations about TPC.

When we make the argument that decentralization of humans is equivalent to assemblage, we miss the concept that assemblage theory doesn’t necessarily care about the essence of the singularities of the assemblage, and therefore allows for a flat ontology by its nature. I think the arguments presented by Johnson-Eilolaa and Selberb definitely make a case for a post-humanist theoretical framework, but don’t fully close the gap between post-humanism and assemblage.

Assemblage theory, however, allows for other theoretical frameworks to exist within the assemblage and therefor can co-exist with sophisticated, but still human centered, rhetorical frameworks like those of Miller and Edbaur, allowing that humans must make decisions, based on perception, to affect change as a response to complex social exigencies. I believe that, when viewing these situations in detail, agency and exigence are a human centric phenomena and likely our ongoing role in the assemblages we encounter.
